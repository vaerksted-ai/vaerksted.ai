The problem: pilots without an owner or a result

Most leadership teams are past the question of whether to use AI. There are licences, a few enthusiastic people and a handful of pilots. What is usually missing is three things: a clear view of where AI changes the result in your own product and technology work, a plan with a name against every item, and one use case that lives in the daily work instead of in a demo.

Without those, pilots stall when the enthusiast gets busy. Tools get used in ways that save ten minutes but do not change how the team decides, builds or ships. And the next budget conversation has nothing to point to.

Advisory fixes that sequence. You leave with a map, a plan and a working use case. Not a slide deck about AI in general.

Who this is for

This is built for people who lead product and technology and have to answer for the result:

  • Chief product officers, chief technology officers, heads of product and heads of engineering
  • Founders who lead product and tech themselves
  • Leadership teams that have already started with AI tools and now need direction, ownership and a first result

It is probably not the right fit if you want a vendor to build or run a system for you, or if you want a general introduction to what AI is. Værksted gives advice and plans, not delivery. For introductions and team skills, Værksted also offers training and workshops for product teams (more on that below).

Two offers, three phases

The work is split into three phases, sold as two separate offers with separate scopes and separate prices. Each phase ends in something you can hold, show to your board and hand to the people who own it.

  • Offer 1, planning: phase 1 (the map) and phase 2 (the plan), plus 2 days of follow-up advice about 30 days later.
  • Offer 2, first use case: phase 3.

They are separate on purpose. Planning is a complete piece of work that stands on its own. The first use case is a different kind of work, done by your team in its daily routine with us guiding, and you should see its cost as its own line.

Phase 1: Map where AI pays off

We go through how your product and technology work is done today: how decisions get made, how work moves from idea to release, and where people spend their time. Then we mark each area one of three ways: AI changes the result, AI only saves time, or it is not worth the effort right now.

Deliverable: a written map of opportunities, ranked, with the reasoning behind each ranking so your team can challenge it.

Phase 2: A plan with owners and measures

A map is not a plan. In this phase we turn the top of the ranking into a short, prioritised plan. Every item gets one owner from your organisation, one measure that tells you whether it worked, and an explicit list of what you will not do this quarter. About 30 days later we follow up with 2 days of advice, so the plan is adjusted while it is in use.

Deliverable: a prioritised plan, with an owner and a measure per item, that your leadership team has agreed to.

Phase 3: A first use case, in use

We pick one item from the plan and get it into use in the team's own work, with the people who will keep using it. Your team does the work and we guide, challenge and unblock. This is the phase that separates advisory from a report. The point is that one real piece of work now works differently, and the team can say why.

Deliverable: a working use case in your own work, and a short written playbook so it can be repeated on the next item.

How an engagement works

  1. Intro call. Book a call. We talk about your team, what has been tried and what you want to be true in six months. If we are not the right fit, we say so on the call.
  2. Scoping. We agree scope, dates and who we work with. You get this in writing before any work begins.
  3. Planning (offer 1). Map and plan, with a review with your leadership team at the end of each phase, and 2 days of follow-up advice about 30 days later.
  4. First use case (offer 2). One item from the plan, in use in your team's work, with a review at the end.
  5. Handover. It ends when the first use case is in use and the plan is in the hands of your owners. We tell you what we would do next, and you decide who carries it out: your own team or a supplier.

What you bring: a sponsor on the leadership team, access to the people who do the work, and one real piece of work to test on. A real piece of work matters more than a perfect one. A use case chosen to look good in a demo teaches nobody anything.

What makes a good first use case

The choice decides whether the rest of the plan is taken seriously. Four questions sort candidates quickly:

  • Is it used in the daily work? A use case that only lives in a workshop does not last.
  • Does it have a named owner? If nobody can say "this is mine", it dies when attention moves.
  • Can you measure it? One number you already track beats a new dashboard.
  • Is it small enough to succeed? Do not pick the most impressive item. Pick the one that can show a result soon and teach you something.

FAQ

What is AI advisory?

AI advisory is senior guidance for a leadership team on where AI will change the result in the business, how to plan that change and who owns each part of it. It differs from a tool rollout because it starts with your work and ends with a plan and a working use case, not a licence count.

How is AI advisory different from AI consulting?

The terms overlap, and firms use them loosely. In how we use them: advisory is guidance and a plan that your own people carry out, with a first use case in use in your team's work. Værksted gives advice and plans. We do not build, ship or run systems for clients, so if you need a supplier to do that, we will say so and you hire one separately.
